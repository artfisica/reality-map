# Case Study III: When Intervention Becomes Destruction and Abandonment

## Afghanistan, Iraq, Syria and What Their Aftermath Warns Venezuela

**By Arturo Sánchez Pineda**  
**20 August 2026**

Foreign military intervention is commonly narrated from the viewpoint of the intervening power. The debate begins with its threat perception, follows its troops and ends when its government declares withdrawal or success. For civilians in the target state, that chronology is false. The loss of relatives, institutions, homes, education and economic security continues long after the intervening power has revised its objectives.

Afghanistan, Iraq and Syria were not one war and do not share a single cause. They should not be collapsed into the statement that the United States alone “destroyed” them. Their comparison supports a more disciplined conclusion. External powers repeatedly used or prolonged force while overstating their ability to engineer political order. They externalised the long-term cost to local populations and retained control over when their own responsibility would be treated as finished.

I am not comparing these societies because Venezuelans are destined to repeat their histories. I compare them because civilians cannot evaluate an intervention only through the intervening state’s stated intention or its first military result. As a physicist I would not extrapolate from systems with different initial conditions without stating the limits. As a Venezuelan I cannot ignore a recurring mechanism merely because the final outcomes will not be identical.

## Scope of the comparison

This study does not try to decide which country was “destroyed most.” It traces the distribution of control and long-term cost: who defined the threat, who chose the use and duration of force, who controlled withdrawal, which institutions remained viable, and which population inherited the consequences.

Evidence of sustainable security, representative government and institutional capacity attributable to an intervention would weaken the general conclusion. Limited improvements in infrastructure, education or health are therefore included rather than suppressed. They did occur. The conclusion is that they did not mature into the durable political order used to justify the scale and duration of intervention.

## Iraq: invasion before peaceful options were exhausted

The 2003 US-led invasion overthrew Saddam Hussein rapidly. The British Iraq Inquiry later found that diplomatic options had not been exhausted, military action was not a last resort and no explicit Security Council authorisation existed. It also found that judgments concerning Iraq’s weapons capabilities were presented with unjustified certainty and that post-conflict planning was inadequate.

Those findings matter because they come from an official inquiry by a principal coalition state. They establish that the catastrophe cannot be explained merely as an unforeseeable failure after a sound decision. The coalition entered without adequately preparing for the society and institutions that would exist after regime collapse.

The invasion and occupation were followed by state breakdown, insurgency, sectarian violence, torture, mass displacement and the later rise of ISIS. Iraq retained a state, elections and significant public institutions; “only destruction” would therefore be inaccurate. The defensible judgment is that the intervention failed to deliver the stable and secure democratic order invoked to justify it, while Iraqi civilians absorbed consequences radically disproportionate to those borne by the states that invaded.

## Afghanistan: two decades of goals without a workable theory

The intervention in Afghanistan began after the attacks of 11 September 2001 and expanded into a twenty-year attempt to defeat armed groups and build a centralised state. The US Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction concluded that the United States struggled to develop and execute a coherent strategy. It imposed unrealistic timelines, rotated personnel too quickly, failed to understand local institutions and often created projects that could not be sustained.

The Taliban’s return to national power in August 2021 exposed the difference between expenditure and durable capacity. Some gains in education, health, infrastructure, media and urban life were real, particularly for women and girls. Their fragility makes the failure more serious, not less. A project advertised as transformation left Afghans with renewed authoritarian rule, severe restrictions on women and girls, displacement and a humanitarian crisis after the principal external power chose its exit.

## Syria: cumulative devastation, not a single invasion story

Syria requires the greatest causal precision. The devastation grew from the Assad government’s repression of protest and conduct of war; armed opposition and extremist groups; ISIS; Russian and Iranian military intervention; US-led operations; Turkish intervention; foreign-backed militias; sanctions; and the division of territory among competing authorities.

No honest account can assign all Syrian destruction to Washington. Nor can an honest account exclude the consequences of US military operations, support to armed partners, territorial involvement, sanctions and strategic decisions. The governing fact is cumulative internationalisation: outside powers pursued bounded security interests while Syrians lived in a fragmented country whose physical, social and political reconstruction none of those powers could or would guarantee.

## The recurring lifecycle

Across the three cases, a pattern appears:

1. an exceptional threat is identified;
2. force is presented as necessary and manageable;
3. rapid military objectives replace a credible political settlement;
4. institutions break or fragment;
5. local armed actors and foreign patrons expand;
6. civilians absorb death, flight and economic collapse;
7. the intervening power narrows its original promises;
8. withdrawal ends its domestic political exposure, not the local crisis.

This pattern is not a mechanical law. It is a risk model. It tells civilians what questions official war plans omit: who will provide security after the initial strike; which institutions survive leadership removal; how armed factions will respond; who maintains utilities and salaries; how sanctions affect reconstruction; and what obligation the intervening state accepts when its priorities change.

The model also has a sequence. Target construction and legal compression usually precede large-scale intervention. That is why the next study begins with the Caribbean killings rather than with the attack on Venezuela: it examines how suspected criminals were first converted into military targets and how lethal force was normalised near Venezuelan territory.

## Venezuela: comparison as warning, not prophecy

The United States used military force in Venezuela on 3 January 2026, captured Nicolás Maduro and threatened further action to compel compliance. Switzerland’s official legal assessment found violations of the prohibition on force, sovereignty, territorial integrity, non-intervention and Maduro’s immunity, and found no valid legal justification.

Venezuela is not Afghanistan, Iraq or Syria. The scale, duration, actors and institutions are different. It would be irresponsible to declare that an identical outcome is inevitable. The comparison concerns architecture:

- an external power constructs a threat in exceptional security terms;
- it claims authority to determine a foreign political outcome;
- force and the threat of renewed force constrain local decisions;
- civilians face economic and institutional consequences they did not choose;
- the external power can later redefine success and reduce its exposure.

The danger is not only additional bombing. It is prolonged strategic dependency: domestic officials making formally Venezuelan decisions within limits enforced by a foreign power capable of attacking again. Such a government may retain legal personality while losing meaningful freedom over security, energy, justice and foreign relations.

This is why precise terminology is essential. The January action was an unlawful military intervention and, in Switzerland’s assessment, an act of aggression. At the date of that assessment it was not a belligerent occupation because the United States did not exercise effective control over Venezuelan territory. Calling it an occupation without proving that threshold would replace analysis with analogy. Calling it a consensual transition would erase the force that structured the result.

## The civilian conclusion

Whatever limited gains occurred in Afghanistan, Iraq or Syria, the larger promises attached to intervention were not fulfilled. The affected populations inherited displacement, damaged institutions and fragmented sovereignty while external powers retained superior control over escalation, resources and exit.

For Venezuelans, the rational response is neither panic nor trust. It is documentation and contingency. Preserve records. Track who controls which institution. Separate an official Venezuelan signature from the coercive environment in which it was produced. Treat renewed-force threats as evidence of constrained autonomy. Reject the fiction that outside powers will necessarily remain to repair the order they disrupt.

Wars end for an intervening power when its government decides that the mission is over. They do not end for the population that inherits the aftermath.

That sentence is analytical, but it is also personal. In *Dolor*, I wrote that it had become difficult to look at the children in my own family without thinking of children who had no opportunity even to survive wars chosen by people with power. A casualty statistic must be comparable and sourced; it must never become a method for making those children abstract. Scientific discipline should protect human meaning from exaggeration, not remove it from the analysis.
