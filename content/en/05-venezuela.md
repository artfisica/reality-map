# Case Study V: Venezuela—When the Threat Became Force

## Unlawful Intervention, Constrained Autonomy and Withdrawal from the ICC

**By Arturo Sánchez Pineda**  
**31 August 2026**

On 3 January 2026, the United States carried out a military intervention in Venezuela, struck sites in Venezuelan territory, captured Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores, and transferred them to the United States. This is not a hypothetical invasion or a prediction. It is a documented use of force whose unlawfulness was set out in unusually direct terms by the Swiss Federal Department of Foreign Affairs.

The event followed months of US maritime killings and military escalation in the Caribbean. That order matters. The preceding case showed how suspected criminals were converted into military targets through executive designation and secret evidence. This case examines the next change of scale: from lethal force against small vessels to direct force against Venezuelan territory, followed by continuing pressure over the decisions of the Venezuelan state.

In *Miedo*, written before the intervention, I described the fear produced by watching powerful governments select which crimes to punish and which to tolerate. In *Dolor*, written afterward, the danger was no longer prospective. These texts establish my standpoint and the chronology of my concern. They do not substitute for evidence. The legal claims below rest on official documents, most importantly Switzerland’s own assessment.

I do not return to those articles to say “I told you so.” I began *Dolor* by rejecting precisely that phrase. I return to them because they distinguish a warning made before the event from a reconstruction written afterward. *Miedo* shows that the concern existed before the attack; it does not by itself prove why the attack occurred. That proof must come from documents independent of my memory and emotion.

The Venezuelan case requires three questions to be kept separate. Was Maduro’s government democratically legitimate? Was the US intervention lawful? Are subsequent acts of the Venezuelan state strategically autonomous? The answer to one does not determine the others.

This separation is the core of the method. It prevents opposition to Maduro from being used as a legal justification for foreign force, and it prevents opposition to foreign force from becoming an excuse to ignore repression by Maduro’s government. A universal standard must survive both tests.

## What Switzerland’s legal assessment establishes

The Swiss memorandum of 13 January 2026 concluded that the US action violated the prohibition on the use of force in Article 2(4) of the UN Charter, Venezuelan sovereignty and territorial integrity, the principle of non-intervention and Maduro’s personal immunity as the effective head of state. It found no armed attack capable of supporting self-defence, no Security Council mandate and no Venezuelan consent.

The memorandum classified the intervention as aggression in the general international-law sense. It also found that continuing credible threats of renewed force, used to compel the acting Venezuelan authorities to meet US demands, themselves violated the prohibition on force and the principle of non-intervention.

The document did not find that Venezuela was under belligerent occupation. Occupation is a factual legal status requiring non-consensual effective control of territory. According to the memorandum, US influence over a Venezuelan government did not at that time equal effective territorial control. The accurate vocabulary is therefore **unlawful intervention, aggression and coercion**, unless later evidence establishes the separate occupation threshold.

Switzerland’s own conduct also reveals a tension. Its lawyers described the intervention clearly, while the Federal Council froze possible Maduro-associated assets after the loss of power under Swiss asset-recovery legislation. The memorandum explained that such a freeze turns on the fact of loss of power, not on whether that loss was lawfully produced. This is a practical example of two institutional logics operating at once: one branch records the violation; another administers the consequences of the new fact created by it.

The memorandum is an official legal assessment, not a judgment of the International Court of Justice. Its value lies in the precision with which a government normally aligned with the broader Western order described the conduct of the United States. The classification should be attributed accurately and not inflated beyond the document.

## Legitimacy does not cancel sovereignty

Maduro’s democratic record and allegations of repression remain legitimate subjects of accountability. But the UN Charter does not grant a foreign state a general right to invade whenever it disputes another government’s legitimacy. If it did, the prohibition on force would become permission for the strongest state to decide which governments deserve protection.

The distinction is not a defence of Maduro. It is a defence of Venezuelans’ right not to have their political order selected through foreign military force. A leader may be repressive, electorally illegitimate or criminally accused while still benefiting from the immunities and sovereign rules that prevent interstate relations from becoming organised abduction.

## From participation in the ICC to withdrawal

Venezuela ratified the Rome Statute in 2000. The ICC Prosecutor opened a preliminary examination concerning Venezuela in February 2018 and formally opened an investigation in November 2021 into alleged crimes against humanity.

The relationship was already adversarial before the 2026 intervention. Venezuela’s legislature approved a move to repeal the ratifying law in December 2025. On 24 July 2026, the Venezuelan government delivered formal notice of withdrawal to the UN Secretary-General. The depositary notice records Venezuela’s stated reasons: selectivity, politicisation, bias toward developing countries and the use of the Court as “lawfare.” The withdrawal takes effect on 24 July 2027.

The chronology also includes the US position. On 13 July 2026, the US State Department announced a campaign to dismantle what it described as the ICC’s threat to American sovereignty and to press states to withdraw. After Venezuela’s notification, the Department publicly welcomed the withdrawal.

This sequence raises an unavoidable question: whose policy did the withdrawal serve? It served the Venezuelan authorities’ pre-existing effort to escape an investigation into alleged crimes. It also served the Trump administration’s announced campaign against the Court. The January intervention and threats of renewed force deprived the surrounding political environment of normal autonomy.

None of that proves that Marco Rubio or another US official directly ordered the Venezuelan withdrawal. The sources establish public pressure and aligned interests, not a private command. The responsible conclusion is that the withdrawal was **an official Venezuelan act taken under conditions of severely constrained strategic autonomy**.

That conclusion is an inference with defined limits. Evidence that the Venezuelan authorities had pursued withdrawal before January weakens any claim that Washington originated the policy. Evidence of the intervention, continuing threats and the US anti-ICC campaign strengthens the conclusion that the final decision occurred in a coercive environment advantageous to both governments. The correct model allows more than one actor to benefit from the same act.

## What withdrawal does not erase

Article 127 of the Rome Statute makes withdrawal prospective. It takes effect one year after notification unless a later date is specified. Withdrawal does not discharge obligations that arose while the state was a party, does not affect cooperation connected to investigations or proceedings begun before the effective date, and does not prejudice the Court’s continued consideration of matters already under examination before withdrawal became effective.

Venezuela’s notice therefore does not abolish the existing ICC situation or retroactively remove the Court’s jurisdiction over alleged crimes committed while the Statute applied. Nor does the Court’s investigation give the United States legal authority to attack Venezuela. International criminal accountability and the prohibition on inter-state force are separate parts of law; one cannot be cancelled in order to defend the other.

The aggression jurisdiction presents another limitation. The Swiss memorandum explained that the ICC did not have jurisdiction over the alleged US crime of aggression in these circumstances: the United States is not a Rome Statute party, Venezuela had not ratified the Kampala aggression amendments, and a Security Council referral was politically implausible. The result is not that the intervention was lawful. It is that a violation may exist without an available court able to prosecute it.

## The civilian status map

The Venezuelan state now has at least three simultaneous characteristics:

- **legal personality:** Venezuela remains a sovereign state and its official acts remain attributable to its authorities;
- **contested legitimacy:** questions of electoral validity, repression and domestic accountability remain unresolved by the foreign intervention;
- **constrained autonomy:** the demonstrated capacity and threatened willingness of the United States to use force narrow the decisions Venezuelan officials can make freely.

These layers explain why “the government decided” is factually incomplete. A signature can be valid as an official act while the range of available decisions has been shaped by illegal coercion. Conversely, coercion does not automatically make every government act legally void or prove that every official is an American proxy. Each decision requires evidence about authorship, pressure, benefit and alternatives.

This is the point at which objectivity and neutrality must be distinguished. Objectivity requires that I state what the evidence cannot prove, even when a stronger accusation would be rhetorically satisfying. Neutrality would require me to behave as if unlawful force and the people subjected to it occupied equivalent positions. They do not.

## From constrained autonomy to de facto tutelage

Evidence published after the Swiss memorandum makes the mechanism more precise. [Executive Order 14373, signed by Donald Trump on 9 January 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/01/safeguarding-venezuelan-oil-revenue-for-the-good-of-the-american-and-venezuelan-people/), defines a category it calls “Foreign Government Deposit Funds”: revenues derived from Venezuelan natural resources that are paid to the US government or held by it in designated Treasury accounts. The order prohibits their transfer, withdrawal or disposition except as authorised by the United States, and directs that the secretary of state determine the instructions governing disbursement. The order states that the money remains the property of Venezuela. That is precisely why its administration from Washington is direct evidence of a material transfer of decision-making capacity, even without a formal transfer of ownership.

A [BBC News Mundo investigation published on 10 August 2026](https://www.bbc.com/mundo/articles/ce8l6xj85ppo) assembled further indicators: US influence over oil marketing, sectoral reforms, sanctions relief, dialogue between government and opposition, and distance from Venezuela’s traditional allies. The article attributed the phrase “suspended sovereignty” to Benigno Alarcón and the description “a form of tutelage” to Colette Capriles. These are characterisations by interviewed specialists, not legal categories established by a court and not facts that must be accepted merely because the BBC reported them.

The characterisations nevertheless have independent support in the executive order, the [US plan for stabilisation, recovery and transition](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2026/01/secretary-of-state-marco-rubio-and-secretary-of-war-pete-hegseth-remarks-to-the-press), and the military capacity demonstrated on 3 January. The observable relationship is asymmetric: Washington controls levers affecting the survival of the interim government and, at the same time, the opposition’s operating space. This makes the United States a veto actor for rival Venezuelan political projects. The simultaneous dependence of both camps does not make the original intervention consensual or turn resignation into freely given consent.

The timeline also requires precision. Later management may operate without daily new attacks, but it should not therefore be described simply as an “unarmed” or “bloodless” intervention. The present structure was preceded and enabled by a military operation that struck Venezuelan territory, captured its authorities and left a credible threat of repetition in place. The correct language distinguishes **the armed origin of the coercion** from **its later administration through economic, diplomatic and institutional controls**.

This evidence strengthens the conclusion of severely constrained autonomy. It does not prove belligerent occupation, US control of every Venezuelan decision or the absence of agency by Delcy Rodríguez, the governing movement or the opposition. “De facto tutelage” can describe the political relationship created by the combined dependencies; the safer legal classifications remain those used in the Swiss memorandum: unlawful intervention, aggression, threat of force and violation of the principle of non-intervention.

## From revenue custody to an announced production stake

On 28 August, Trump and Rodríguez announced a new energy agreement concerning 17 Venezuelan fields with a stated proven potential of 65 billion barrels. Both governments publicised projections of US$100 billion in investment and more than US$209 billion in Venezuelan tax and royalty revenue. Those matching figures establish that an agreement was announced. They do not establish its complete legal or commercial terms: as of 31 August, neither government had published the agreement itself.

The [Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/venezuela-oil-trump-deal-unknown-questions-reserves-c229bc39b6e1a3d5dd16f7f9e67fef3f), on the basis of an unnamed US official, that a new company formed with an unnamed private operator received 100-year development rights and that the United States would obtain 55 per cent of its “effective output,” combining an ownership stake with a right to buy oil at cost. The same official said purchased oil would supply the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve and the military. The [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/29/world/americas/venezuela-oil-us-takeover.html), citing people familiar with the arrangement, identified the operator as a firm affiliated with Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt López. These are important attributed reports; without the contract, they cannot be restated as publicly verified clauses.

Rodríguez subsequently gave a different duration: 25 years, not 100. She said Venezuela retained ownership and sovereignty over the resources, set a production objective above 1.5 million barrels per day, described minimum royalties of 16 per cent and an income-tax rate of 34 per cent, and estimated that about US$19 of each barrel sold at the assumed US$65 reference price would enter Venezuela. A state can retain legal title to oil in the ground while another actor acquires extensive operational, purchasing and revenue rights. “Venezuelan ownership” and “US majority control” are therefore not necessarily logical opposites, but the two governments’ incompatible duration claims cannot be reconciled from the published record.

The constitutional baseline is clearer than the contract. Article 12 of the [Venezuelan Constitution](https://www.asambleanacional.gob.ve/storage/documentos/botones/constitucion-nacional-20191205135853.PDF) makes hydrocarbon deposits property of the Republic, in the public domain and inalienable. Article 150 requires National Assembly approval for national public-interest contracts with foreign states, official foreign entities or companies not domiciled in Venezuela. The unpublished instrument may use a corporate structure that changes which rules apply; that is precisely why its text, approvals, operator identity and allocation of control must be public before legality or national benefit can be audited.

The evidentiary update is substantial. US control is no longer confined to Treasury custody of specified revenue. The announced structure reaches production, corporate participation, at-cost purchasing and strategic and military supply. It still does not prove that the United States owns Venezuela’s oil in the ground, controls all national production or has lawfully acquired the rights it claims.

## What Venezuelan civilians should monitor

The practical indicators are not diplomatic adjectives. They are control points:

- appointments in security, energy, justice and foreign-policy institutions;
- the complete text, approvals, duration, operator and beneficial owners of every oil agreement;
- the division of production, purchasing rights, prices, costs, taxes, royalties and revenue;
- presence or command authority of foreign military or intelligence personnel;
- conditions attached to sanctions relief, financial access and recognition;
- arrests, transfers or prosecutions requested by foreign authorities;
- changes to treaty participation and judicial cooperation;
- credible threats of renewed force tied to specific Venezuelan decisions;
- whether official texts and negotiations are public and subject to domestic review.

For Venezuelans abroad, criticism of documented state conduct is not extremism. It should nevertheless be organised with evidentiary discipline: preserve primary documents, distinguish legal findings from political judgments, avoid asserting secret control without proof and do not confuse hostility to a government with hostility to a people.

[Case Study VI](../externalisation/) examines a different and now better-supported connection: Washington is presenting claimed control over Venezuelan oil as a promise of lower US fuel prices and strategic supply while the Iran war keeps energy costs elevated before the November elections. There is still no transaction evidence that Venezuelan revenue is paying US federal debt. That limit remains true, but it is no longer the principal meaning of the oil record.

## Conclusion

The central fact is not that the United States might invade Venezuela. It did use military force in Venezuela on 3 January 2026. The central legal conclusion is not a partisan Venezuelan claim. Switzerland’s foreign ministry found multiple violations of international law and no valid justification.

The central political question is what sovereignty means after that act. Venezuela remains formally sovereign, but its government operates beneath a demonstrated threat of renewed force. Its ICC withdrawal is attributable to Venezuela and aligned with its own pre-existing interest in escaping scrutiny. It is also aligned with the Trump administration’s campaign against the Court and was completed under coercive conditions created by that administration.

The proper classification is neither normal sovereign choice nor proven foreign command. It is **formal decision under constrained autonomy**.

The oil announcement gives that abstraction a measurable structure. Washington claims majority control, at-cost access and strategic supply; Caracas claims continuing ownership and a 25-year development bargain; neither has published the document capable of resolving their conflicting descriptions. Sovereignty cannot be audited through slogans from either capital. It must be tested against the contract, the approvals, the cash flows and who can say no.

I write this as a Venezuelan who has opposed the abuses of Venezuelan rulers and who also rejects the proposition that a foreign military may decide our political future. Those positions are not contradictory. They are the minimum conditions of a universal rule: no government acquires immunity through anti-imperial rhetoric, and no great power acquires a right of intervention by correctly identifying another government’s crimes.
