# International Law for Adversaries, Indulgence for Allies

## Europe and Switzerland’s Gaza Double Standard—and the Lebanon Enforcement Ceiling

**By Arturo Sánchez Pineda**  
**22 August 2026**

*The first case study in **The Civilian Geopolitical Reality Map**—a framework for understanding states through their observed conduct, capabilities and interests rather than through their declarations alone.*

I approach this comparison as a Venezuelan and as a physicist and engineer. The first position determines why selective enforcement is not abstract to me. The second requires me to define the objects being compared, preserve material differences between the cases and identify where the record supports a fact, a legal conclusion or an inference. Objectivity here means a reproducible method, not emotional distance from civilian suffering.

I am not writing to say “I told you so,” or to place myself outside the cowardice I criticise. In *Miedo*, I included myself among those who look away, fear the personal consequences and still do far too little. In *Dolor*, I wrote that “I told you so” would be repugnant in the face of people who had lost their homes, families or parts of their bodies. This study does not seek moral superiority. It identifies the point at which governmental fear becomes silence, silence becomes political protection, and that protection is paid for through other people’s suffering.

On 6 August 2026, the European Union welcomed an agreement providing for the disarmament of Hamas and other non-state armed groups, the full withdrawal of Israeli forces from Gaza, and a transition to civilian government. If fully implemented, it may finally end the immediate catastrophe. It does not answer the question Europe and Switzerland have avoided for nearly three years: why did governments that mobilised sanctions, finance and political capital so rapidly for Ukraine refuse to apply comparable pressure while Gaza was being destroyed?

This is not an argument against defending Ukraine. Russia’s invasion was an unlawful act of aggression, and supporting Ukraine’s right to resist it was justified. The issue is whether the international law invoked for Ukraine is universal, or whether it is enforced only when doing so coincides with Western strategic interests.

The evidence supports the latter explanation more strongly. Europe and Switzerland were not legally helpless in relation to Gaza. They possessed economic, diplomatic and legal instruments capable of imposing meaningful costs. They largely chose not to use them. This conclusion would be weakened by evidence that the instruments were legally unavailable, administratively impossible or applied comparably to Israel; the institutional record instead shows proposals, precedents and selective refusal.

## A method for reading state behaviour

This study applies the manual's full [six-layer power map](../../method/#2-the-six-layer-power-map). Three layers do most of the comparative work here:

1. **The dependency:** Which alliances, financial systems, technologies, energy supplies or protecting powers limit freedom of action?
2. **The instrument:** Which available tools—sanctions, trade, weapons, diplomacy, courts or humanitarian aid—are used or deliberately withheld?
3. **The cost:** Who benefits from the decision, and who is made to absorb its consequences?

The rule and the public declaration remain necessary controls, while the protected interest helps explain the choice. The comparison becomes visible when those stated commitments are tested against dependency, instrument and cost.

## What has—and has not—been legally determined

Precision matters, particularly when discussing genocide.

In September 2025, the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory concluded that Israeli authorities and Israeli security forces had committed and were continuing to commit genocide against Palestinians in Gaza. The Commission found both genocidal acts and the specific intent required by the Genocide Convention. This was not a slogan from a protest movement. It was the conclusion of an investigative body mandated by the UN Human Rights Council, based on an extensive evidentiary record.

Separately, the International Court of Justice has issued binding provisional measures in *South Africa v. Israel*. The Court ordered Israel to prevent acts falling within the Genocide Convention, prevent and punish direct and public incitement to genocide, preserve evidence, and enable urgently needed humanitarian assistance. In May 2024, it ordered Israel to halt its offensive and other action in Rafah insofar as it could inflict conditions of life capable of bringing about the physical destruction of the Palestinian group in whole or in part.

As of 20 August 2026, however, the ICJ has not issued its final judgment on whether Israel bears state responsibility for genocide. That distinction must be stated honestly. The UN Commission has reached a genocide determination; the final and legally binding ICJ decision on the merits remains pending.

But the absence of a final judgment is not an excuse for inaction. Under the ICJ’s 2007 judgment in *Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro*, the obligation to prevent genocide arises when a state learns, or should normally have learned, of a serious risk that genocide will be committed. It does not begin only after a court confirms that the genocide has already occurred. The measures expected of a state depend on its capacity to influence the actors concerned.

By early 2024, the serious risk was impossible to deny. By 2025, the record included binding ICJ orders, mass killing and displacement, the systematic destruction of civilian infrastructure, severe restrictions on food and medicine, allegations of starvation as a method of warfare, and repeated warnings from UN agencies. Waiting for a final judgment while retaining normal relations was a political choice, not a legal requirement.

The International Criminal Court has also issued arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant. The judges found reasonable grounds to believe that they bore responsibility for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity, including starvation as a method of warfare, murder, persecution and other inhumane acts. These are warrants, not convictions. Criminal responsibility is individual: it applies to accused officials, not to Israelis or Jewish people collectively. Rejecting collective punishment of Palestinians requires rejecting collective blame in the other direction as well.

## The EU documented the violations and then failed to act decisively

The European Union cannot credibly claim that it lacked information or a legal basis for action.

Article 2 of the EU–Israel Association Agreement makes respect for human rights and democratic principles an essential element of the relationship. In June 2025, the EU’s own review found indications that Israel was breaching its obligations under that provision. The underlying assessment recorded a complete blockade of aid for eleven weeks, mass displacement, attacks causing unprecedented civilian casualties, the systematic degradation of hospitals and serious concerns regarding starvation, collective punishment and attacks on humanitarian operations.

In September 2025, the European Commission went further. It formally proposed suspending trade-related provisions of the Association Agreement. Its legal analysis concluded that Israel’s violation of Article 2 constituted a serious material breach and that partial suspension was an appropriate and proportionate response. The Commission stated that the urgency arose from the blockade, widespread famine, human-rights violations and the humanitarian catastrophe created by Israel’s military intervention.

The legal case was therefore prepared by the EU itself. Yet member states did not produce the political agreement necessary to deploy the Union’s full economic leverage.

Sanctions under the EU’s Common Foreign and Security Policy require unanimity. Member states did not reach the political agreement required for broad collective measures despite the Union’s own legal assessment and proposals. The institutional result was paralysis. This wording identifies the documented mechanism without assigning motives to individual capitals that public records alone cannot prove.

That explains why broader EU-wide coercive measures failed. It does not absolve the member states. Within the limits of EU law, they retained authority over arms-export licences, diplomatic relations, areas of public procurement, criminal cooperation and bilateral military or institutional relationships. Institutional paralysis became a convenient way to disperse responsibility among 27 governments until no government appeared responsible for the collective result.

The EU did provide substantial humanitarian assistance, sanction Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and impose targeted measures on a small number of violent settlers and activists obstructing aid. Those actions matter. But humanitarian aid is not a substitute for stopping the policies that create the humanitarian emergency. Funding food while declining to impose adequate consequences on the authority restricting that food is crisis management, not prevention.

## Switzerland’s neutrality does not explain its choices

Switzerland’s conduct makes the double standard particularly visible.

The Swiss Embargo Act is not limited to mandatory UN sanctions. It permits the Confederation to implement sanctions ordered by the United Nations, the OSCE or Switzerland’s most significant trading partners. The Federal Council’s own July 2026 factsheet states that economic sanctions are, in principle, compatible with neutrality. Neutrality law neither generally requires nor prohibits them.

Switzerland therefore had a legal route to adopt the EU’s sanctions against violent Israeli settlers. In September 2025, Parliament expressly considered and rejected doing so. It also rejected suspending the free-trade agreement with Israel and ending military cooperation.

Compare that response with Russia. By February 2026, Switzerland had adopted the EU’s nineteenth sanctions package and maintained an asset-freeze list identical to the EU’s, covering approximately 2,600 individuals, companies and organisations. The measures extended across finance, energy, cryptocurrency, services, technology, trade and diplomatic movement. Switzerland was willing to use its position in the international financial system to help raise the cost of Russian aggression.

Against supporters of Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, Switzerland likewise adopted EU financial and travel sanctions under the Embargo Act. Against violent Israeli settlers, it declined to use the same legal mechanism. Against the Israeli state, it maintained the free-trade relationship and rejected an end to military cooperation.

This asymmetry was not imposed by neutrality. It was produced by political selection.

Switzerland did condemn settlement expansion, forced displacement, restrictions on humanitarian assistance and Israel’s unlawful occupation of Palestinian territory. It supported ceasefire demands and humanitarian initiatives. But it repeatedly chose declaratory diplomacy over coercive measures. Calling that position neutral does not make it neutral. It means that the costs of Swiss restraint were borne primarily by Palestinians rather than by Switzerland or its partners.

## Lebanon: recognition without consequence

Lebanon shows that the enforcement problem did not end at Gaza’s border. It also clarifies why saying that Europe and Switzerland “did nothing” is less accurate—and less devastating—than identifying what they actually did.

### The exploding-device attacks

On 17 and 18 September 2024, explosive material hidden in thousands of pagers and handheld radios detonated across Lebanon and in Syria. [Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2024/09/lebanon-establish-international-investigation-into-deadly-attacks-using-exploding-portable-devices/) documented at least 37 deaths and more than 2,931 injuries, including children, medical workers and other civilians. Its evidence laboratory verified explosions in homes, residential streets, grocery stores and a funeral. The devices did not explode in a controlled battlespace; they exploded wherever the person carrying them happened to be and beside whoever happened to be near.

Amnesty’s legal conclusion was conditional and must remain so. It stated that, **if Israeli responsibility were established**, the inability to know the location, holder and surrounding civilians at detonation indicated an indiscriminate attack that would be unlawful under international humanitarian law and should be investigated as war crimes. It also identified the prohibition on booby-traps made to resemble apparently harmless portable objects. This is an attributed legal assessment and a demand for investigation, not a criminal conviction.

The [UN High Commissioner for Human Rights](https://lebanon.un.org/en/278971-comment-un-high-commissioner-human-rights-volker-t%C3%BCrk-explosions-across-lebanon-and-syria) likewise stated that simultaneously targeting thousands of people without knowing who possessed the devices, where they were or who surrounded them violated international human-rights law and, where applicable, international humanitarian law. He called for an independent, thorough and transparent investigation. At the time of Amnesty’s report, the Israeli government had not officially accepted responsibility; Lebanese authorities and US officials attributed the operation to Israel, while Israeli statements were interpreted as implicit acknowledgement. Responsibility should not be converted into a judicially established fact that the cited proceedings have not produced.

The incident belongs in this atlas because it reveals a method as well as a casualty count. Everyday communications equipment was converted into a distributed weapons system whose time of detonation, but not civilian environment, could be controlled. The principal international response recorded here was a demand for investigation. The gap between the gravity of the legal assessment and the weakness of the consequence is another instance of recognition without deterrence.

The destruction is measurable. The World Bank estimated that the conflict from October 2023 to December 2024 produced US$6.8 billion in physical damage, US$7.2 billion in economic losses and US$11 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs. Housing alone accounted for an estimated US$4.6 billion in damage. The Bank estimated a 7.1 per cent contraction of Lebanese GDP in 2024, deepening a collapse that had already reduced the economy severely since 2019.

The renewed regional war in 2026 intensified the human cost. Switzerland reported in July that, by the end of June, more than 700,000 people had been displaced inside Lebanon, more than 4,200 had been killed and more than 12,000 injured since March. It allocated humanitarian funds to Lebanon and the region, pursued diplomatic contacts and offered good offices.

The European Union also acted. It demanded a ceasefire, called for respect for Lebanese sovereignty and international humanitarian law, supported the Lebanese Armed Forces, condemned attacks on the UN Interim Force in Lebanon, demanded Hezbollah’s withdrawal and disarmament, and called on Israel to withdraw from Lebanese territory. These were not meaningless positions. Hezbollah’s attacks, armed autonomy and violations must be named; they do not erase Israel’s independent duties toward civilians, UN personnel or Lebanese territory.

Yet the EU statement of 6 June 2026 announced no coercive measures against Israel comparable to those used against Russia. The pattern was again visible: condemnation, humanitarian finance, security assistance to the weakened state and support for eventual reconstruction, without equivalent political and economic costs imposed on the protected attacking state.

UN peacekeepers were themselves endangered. In October 2024, UNIFIL reported that explosions near an observation tower injured two peacekeepers and that an Israeli military vehicle hit perimeter walls at another position. Later EU reporting recorded further peacekeeper deaths and condemned attacks on the mission. Attribution must remain incident-specific: not every fatal projectile was publicly assigned to an actor when first reported. The established point is that even attacks affecting a UN-mandated force did not trigger an enforcement response capable of deterring repetition.

Europe and Switzerland therefore did not ignore Lebanon. They **socialised the humanitarian cost of the war while refusing to impose comparable political and economic costs on the state inflicting much of the destruction**. Their response was containment without deterrence: condemn the attacks, finance the victims, support reconstruction and preserve the political relationship with the attacking state.

This is **recognition without consequence**. It is more institutionally sophisticated than silence and may keep people alive. It also allows the cause of the emergency to remain protected while European taxpayers and Lebanese civilians absorb different parts of its cost.

## Why Ukraine was different

The different treatment of Ukraine and Gaza is not difficult to explain. It is difficult to defend.

Russia is a strategic adversary and a direct security threat to Europe. Supporting Ukraine weakens that adversary, protects Europe’s eastern security architecture and reinforces the rule that European borders cannot be changed by military force. European governments were therefore willing to accept economic costs, energy disruption and escalation risks.

Israel is a strategic partner protected by the United States. It is integrated into Western defence, intelligence, cybersecurity, research and trade structures. Serious pressure on Israel risks confrontation not merely with the Israeli government, but with Washington—the security guarantor on which most European states continue to depend.

Israel’s territorial size and population are therefore misleading measures of its effective power. Its leverage derives from its place within a much larger American-led system, together with its own military, technological and intelligence capabilities. Europe was not being coerced by a small state acting alone. European governments were choosing not to confront a protected component of their own strategic order.

Historical responsibility also played an important role, especially in Germany and Austria. The obligation to confront antisemitism and protect Jewish life is non-negotiable. But protection of Jewish people cannot mean immunity for the policies of an Israeli government. Too often, those distinct questions were blurred, making demands for sanctions or enforcement of international judgments politically suspect even when they were directed at state conduct and named officials rather than at Jews or Israelis collectively.

Finally, Palestinians possess little coercive leverage. They cannot threaten European energy security, financial markets, elections or military alliances. The political price of inaction is therefore paid overwhelmingly outside Europe. That makes expressions of concern inexpensive and meaningful action costly—the exact environment in which declared principles are most likely to be abandoned.

## International law for adversaries, indulgence for allies

None of this means Europe and Switzerland could have ended the Gaza catastrophe unilaterally or instantaneously. Israel is a sovereign military power, the United States could have countered European pressure, and Hamas remained responsible for the crimes of 7 October and for violations committed during the conflict.

But inability to guarantee an outcome is not the same as inability to act. The EU was Israel’s largest trading partner. Switzerland was a significant financial and commercial actor. Both possessed measures capable of altering incentives:

- suspending trade preferences conditioned on respect for human rights;
- imposing targeted asset freezes and travel bans on officials credibly implicated in grave violations;
- halting arms and dual-use transfers where there was a serious risk of their use in international crimes;
- suspending military, scientific and institutional cooperation linked to the conduct at issue;
- enforcing ICC obligations without inventing special immunity for allied leaders;
- supporting international investigations and protecting them from political retaliation;
- distinguishing in all commercial relations between Israel and the occupied Palestinian territory;
- making restoration of normal relations conditional on compliance with binding international orders.

These are state instruments, not protest slogans. Europe and Switzerland used comparable instruments extensively against Russia. Their reluctance to use them against Israel reveals the operative hierarchy.

The rules are presented as universal, but the consequences are not. Adversaries encounter sanctions, isolation and demands for accountability. Allies receive dialogue, expressions of concern and additional time. Their victims receive humanitarian assistance designed to alleviate suffering that Western governments decline to confront at its source.

Politically, sustaining normal relations while financing the consequences can reasonably be described as complicity in the ordinary sense of enabling a protected policy environment. **Legal complicity in genocide is a separate and demanding category** that has not been adjudicated against European states or Switzerland. The separate duty to prevent does not require a prior finding of complicity or a final judgment that genocide has been completed. It requires states with influence to use the means reasonably available to them when a serious risk becomes known.

The question is therefore not whether Europe and Switzerland knew. Their own institutions documented the conduct. It is not whether they possessed legal authority. Their own laws and proposals identified available measures. Nor is it whether economic pressure is compatible with neutrality. Switzerland’s own government says that it is.

The question is why the law produced coercive action for Ukraine and largely declaratory action for Gaza.

The answer is that international law did not determine the difference. Strategic interest did.

Europe and Switzerland were not helpless. They were selective. They defended the international legal order where doing so aligned with their security architecture, and retreated into procedure, neutrality and diplomatic language where enforcement would have required confronting Israel and the United States.

## What civilians should learn from this case

This case produces a set of practical rules for reading future crises.

First, do not treat a government’s commitment to international law as a prediction of its conduct. Determine whether enforcement supports or threatens its alliances and material interests.

Second, measure policy through instruments rather than vocabulary. “Concern,” “restraint” and “dialogue” may accompany either meaningful pressure or deliberate inaction. Asset freezes, export controls, access to finance, military cooperation and trade preferences reveal priorities more reliably than diplomatic language.

Third, use comparison cases. A state’s rapid mobilisation against one violator establishes what it is institutionally capable of doing. If it later claims helplessness in a comparable case, examine what changed: usually not the available law or administrative machinery, but the identity of the violator and its protecting network.

Fourth, distinguish a legal entitlement from an enforceable guarantee. A civilian may possess rights under international law while living under institutions with little ability or willingness to enforce them. That distinction should inform decisions about personal security, travel, residence, financial exposure, professional obligations and reliance on diplomatic protection.

Fifth, identify where costs are externalised. Governments can maintain a principled public image when the human consequences of their restraint fall on distant populations. Policies often change only when those costs reach domestic elections, markets, security or institutional legitimacy.

Finally, avoid both naïveté and fatalism. States are powerful but not omnipotent or perfectly coherent. Courts, journalists, civil servants, voters, professional bodies and organised civic pressure can alter the political price of inaction. The purpose of a realistic map is not to teach submission. It is to show where power actually operates so that individual and collective action can be directed at the mechanisms that matter.

That is not neutrality. It is strategic subordination—and it has imposed a devastating human cost.

The wider manual, *The Civilian Geopolitical Reality Map*, carries this method into territorial administration, the aftermath of intervention, Venezuela’s sovereignty under coercion and the US maritime killings in the Caribbean and Pacific. The same rule governs every case: measure states through the instruments and costs they select, not only through the principles they announce.

I do not claim a position outside this system. In *Miedo*, I included myself among those who fear the professional and personal cost of speaking while other people bear the physical cost of state violence. In *Dolor*, that fear had become more concrete. This study is the analytical continuation of those texts: less immediate in form, but not less personal in purpose. Its standard applies to Israel and the United States, to Russia, and to the Venezuelan governments of Hugo Chávez and Nicolás Maduro. Selectivity cannot be criticised selectively.
